\chapter{Introduction}
    \section{Unsteady Stokes Flow}
The motion of an incompressible Newtonian fluid is governed by the Navier-Stokes
equations, obtained from the Cauchy momentum equation together with the
incompressibility constraint \parencite{BirdStewartLightfoot2002},

\vspace{0.5cm}
\begin{equation}
    \rho\left(\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t} + (\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\right)
    = -\nabla p + \eta{\nabla}^{2} \boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{f}_{ext},
    \qquad
    \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u} = 0,
    \label{eq:navier-stokes}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent where \(\boldsymbol{u}\) is the velocity field, \(p\) the pressure, \(\rho\) the fluid
density, \(\eta\) the dynamic viscosity, and \(\boldsymbol{f}_{ext}\) an external body force.
The relative importance of the inertial (advective) term \((\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}\)
against the viscous term \(\eta\nabla^2\boldsymbol{u}\) is measured by the Reynolds number,
\(Re = \rho U L / \eta\), for characteristic velocity \(U\) and length scale \(L\)
\parencite{BirdStewartLightfoot2002}. For \(Re \ll 1\), the advective term can be
neglected, reducing \eqref{eq:navier-stokes} to the unsteady Stokes equations,

\vspace{0.5cm}
\begin{equation}
    \rho \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t} = -\nabla p + { \eta\nabla}^{2} \boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{f}_{ext},
    \qquad
    \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u} = 0.
    \label{eq:unsteady}
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent Note that, unlike the steady Stokes flow condition commonly used to
describe low-Reynolds-number flows \((Re \approx 0)\), the time-derivative term \(\rho\,\partial\boldsymbol{u}/\partial t\)
is retained here, while dropping the advective nonlinearity term. Equation
\eqref{eq:unsteady} therefore remains valid for flows driven by time-dependent
boundary conditions or external force, which is the regime of interest in this work.
For example, the transient start-up of pressure-driven flow, oscillatory or
pulsatile forcing, and the low-Reynolds-number swimming or transport of
particles in a viscous medium, where the surrounding fluid does not reach a
steady state on the timescale of the motion of interest.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Physics-informed Gaussian processes (PIGP) have recently been proposed as a
probabilistic alternative to mesh-based solvers (e.g. FEM) and neural-network-based
solvers (e.g. PINNs) for such flow problems. \textcite{Molina2023} introduced the
\emph{Stokesian process} (SP), in which the linearity of the steady Stokes equations \(
    0 = -\nabla p + \eta{\nabla}^{2} \boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{f}_{ext}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u} = 0,\) 
is used to construct a Gaussian process whose covariance kernel is derived directly from the governing
equations, rather than imposed as a soft penalty in a loss function as is done for
PINNs. This construction guarantees that the inferred velocity and pressure fields
satisfy the governing equations exactly at every training point, and
provides a principled estimate of prediction uncertainty, at the cost of solving a
purely \emph{steady} flow problem.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Independently, \textcite{Raissi2018} introduced \emph{numerical Gaussian processes},
in which a Gaussian process prior is placed on the solution of a time-dependent
partial differential equation at each time step, with the covariance kernel obtained
by encoding a chosen time-discretization scheme (e.g. backward Euler, Runge--Kutta)
directly into the kernel. The solution is then advanced in time by treating the
\emph{posterior mean} at time step \(t-1\) as \emph{artificial training data} for the inference at time step \(t\), 
avoiding any spatial mesh or discretization of the spatial differential operators.

\vspace{0.3cm}

This work combines these two approaches to solve the \emph{unsteady} Stokes flow
problem of \eqref{eq:unsteady}: the spatial structure of the Gaussian process kernel
follows the Stokesian process construction of \textcite{Molina2023}, while the
time-marching scheme, using a backward Euler discretization and artificially
generated training data at each step, follows \textcite{Raissi2018}. The full
derivation of the combined kernel and inference procedure is given in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}, and is applied to pressure-driven flow between parallel
plates and through a sinusoidal channel in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Gaussian Process Regression}

Gaussian Process or GP are defined by a series of infinite value that have a 
joint Gaussian distribution.
A GP for function \(f(x)\), with the input locations at \(\boldsymbol{X} =[\boldsymbol{x_1},\boldsymbol{x_2},...,\boldsymbol{x_N}] \), will provide values for
\(\boldsymbol{f(X)}=[\boldsymbol{f(x_1)},\boldsymbol{f(x_2)},...,\boldsymbol{f(x_N)}]\). The joint distribution is in the form of a multi-variate Gaussian

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    p(\boldsymbol{f(X)} | \boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{\theta},\mu(\cdot),k(\cdot,\cdot)) = 
    \mathcal{N}\left(
        \mu(\boldsymbol{X}),k\boldsymbol{(X,X;\theta)}
    \right)
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent \(\mu\) and \(k\) represent the mean function and kernel function respectively

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    \mu(\boldsymbol{X})=\langle f(\boldsymbol{X}) \rangle \equiv \langle f \rangle (\boldsymbol{x})
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    k\boldsymbol(X,X;\theta)=\langle \delta f(\boldsymbol{x}) \delta f(\boldsymbol{x^{'}}) \rangle \equiv \langle \delta f\delta f (\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{'}}) \rangle
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent where \(\theta\) is the hyperparameter of the kernel function. The kernel function used was a square expotential kernel.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    k(x,x^{'})=\exp \left( -\frac{(x-x^{'})^2}{2l^2} + \gamma\right)
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

Here, \(l\) and \(\gamma\) are the hyperparameters to the kernel function, controlling the
length scale and amplitude of the correlations, respectively \parencite{Molina2023}.
To capture the dimensionality of the problem, derived kernels were utilized such as
the product kernel, to calculate the kernel function. 

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    k^{\times}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{'}})
        = \exp\left(\gamma - \frac{(x-x^{'})^2}{2l_x^2} - \frac{(y-y^{'})^2}{2l_y^2}\right)
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent so that each base kernel carries three hyperparameters, \(\gamma _x, \gamma _y\).We can learn the function by inferring the value at each test positions \(X_{\star}\). For a function \(f\),

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{f^{\star}} \\
        \boldsymbol{f} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
        \sim
    \mathcal{N}\left(
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \boldsymbol{\mu^{\star}} \\
            \boldsymbol{\mu} \\
        \end{bmatrix}
        ,
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \boldsymbol{K_{\star \star}} & \boldsymbol{K_{\star}}\\
            \boldsymbol{K^{T}_{\star}}  & \boldsymbol{K} \\
        \end{bmatrix}
    \right)
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent the distribution of \(\boldsymbol{f_{\star}}\ | \boldsymbol{f} 
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}\left(
        \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{K_{\star}}\boldsymbol{K^{-1}}\delta \boldsymbol{f} ,  \boldsymbol{K_{\star \star}} - \boldsymbol{K_{\star}}\boldsymbol{K^{-1}}\boldsymbol{K^{T}_{\star}}
    \right)\) 

\noindent Following \textcite{Molina2023}, the kernel hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are treated 
via a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate rather than full Bayesian marginalization,

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[\log p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{X}, f(\boldsymbol{X}), I)\right]
\end{equation}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent where the posterior is given by \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{X},f(\boldsymbol{X}),I) \propto p(f(\boldsymbol{X})|\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{\theta},I)p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|I)\), 
following the same construction as \textcite{Molina2023}. 

\vspace{0.3cm}

At each timestep, the optimization is carried out using a Nelder--Mead downhill simplex 
method over 100 iterations, matching the approach of \textcite{Molina2023}. The nature of our hyperparameters, 
and stabilization factor of the optimization method will be discussed in full details in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}

\vspace{0.3cm}

Finally, this framework is extended from an arbitrary function \(f\) to the velocity
and pressure fields of \eqref{eq:unsteady} by exploiting the closure of Gaussian
processes under linear operators: since the Stokes momentum balance and continuity
equations are linear in \(\boldsymbol{u}\) and \(p\), any linear combination of their
derivatives (e.g. \(\nabla p\), \(\nabla^2\boldsymbol{u}\), \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\)) is
itself a Gaussian process, jointly correlated with \(\boldsymbol{u}\) and \(p\) through
kernels obtained by applying the corresponding differential operators to
\(k^\times\) \parencite{Molina2023}. This is what allows the governing equations to be
imposed \emph{exactly} at a finite set of training points, rather than approximately
through a loss function. The full construction, including its extension to the
unsteady case via backward Euler time-stepping, is given in the next chapter.

\chapter{Methodology}%
\label{ch:methodology}

The challenge in solving this unsteady flow system is capturing the time propagation of velocity and pressure. 
In this chapter, we derived a backward Euler time discretization of the unsteady Stokes equations (Section~\ref{sec:time-disc}), 
then built a physics-informed Gaussian process kernel that encodes both this time discretization and the steady Stokes equations. 
Section~\ref{sec:periodic-channel} goes deeper into the construction to the periodic channel-flow geometry used in this work, including
how uncertainty from earlier time steps is propagated forward (\textcite{Raissi2018}) and how the kernel hyperparameters are optimized. 
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:inference-algorithm} describes the full time-marching inference algorithm.

\section{Time Discretization}%
\label{sec:time-disc}

There are several methods to capture time-stepping in the governing equations, but for the particular case considered here
where boundary conditions are known at every time step, while only the initial velocity is known. The implicait backward Euler
method was used, in reference to \textcite{Raissi2018}. Doing so, \(u^t\) can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    u^t = u^{t-1} + \frac{\Delta t}{\rho} \left({-\nabla p^t +  \eta{\nabla}^{2} u^t + f_{ext}}\right)
\end{equation}

\noindent Rearranging the equation yields, 

\begin{equation}
    u^t + \frac{\Delta t}{\rho} \left({\nabla p^t -  \eta{\nabla}^{2} u^t}\right)  = u^{t-1} + \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}f_{ext}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, the \(LHS\) of the equation is defined as \({G^{t}}\) where

\begin{equation}
    {G^{t}} \equiv u^t + \frac{\Delta t}{\rho} \left({\nabla p^t -  \eta{\nabla}^{2} u^t}\right)
    \label{eq:governing-eq}
\end{equation}

\noindent Equation\eqref{eq:governing-eq} represent the governing equation of unknown velocity at time \(t\) (\(u^t\)) 
in relation to the previous velocity at time \(t-1\) (\(u^{t-1}\)) and constant external force (\(f_{ext}\)). 
The value of \({G^t}\) at time \(t\) can be calculated using the value of \(u^{t-1}\) and \(f_{ext}\),
which will play a distinct role in predicitng unknown velocity at time \(t\) (\(u^t\)) from known velocity at time \(t-1\) (\(u^{t-1}\)).
Throughout the work, we employed \(\rho\), and \(\eta\) to be equal to 1 to ease the calcuation.

\section{Physics-Informed Kernel Construction}%
\label{sec:kernel-construction}

\textcite{Molina2023} developed the Stokesian process, in which the linearity of the
steady Stokes equations is used to construct a Gaussian process kernel from the
governing equations directly, together with the incompressibility constraint

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{s} = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u}
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}

\noindent For a channel flow problem, where the driving force is a single-axis
pressure gradient, the velocity at the walls and the pressure at the inlet and outlet are known.
Given training data and unknown test locations
\(\boldsymbol{X_\star} = [\boldsymbol{x_{1,\star}},\boldsymbol{x_{2,\star}},...,\boldsymbol{x_{N,\star}}]\),
with unknown velocity and pressure denoted \(\boldsymbol{u^\star}\) and
\(\boldsymbol{p^\star}\), the joint Gaussian process over the unknowns and the training quantities is

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{u^{\star}} \\
        \boldsymbol{p^{\star}} \\
        \boldsymbol{u} \\
        \boldsymbol{p} \\
        \boldsymbol{{G}} \\
        \boldsymbol{s}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K_{\star \star}} & \boldsymbol{Q}\\
        \boldsymbol{Q^T} & \boldsymbol{K} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{K} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K_{u u}} &\boldsymbol{K_{u p}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u G}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u s}} \\
        \ & \boldsymbol{K_{p p}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p G}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p s}} \\
        \ & & \boldsymbol{K_{G G}} & \boldsymbol{K_{G s}} \\
        \ & & & \boldsymbol{K_{s s}} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

To infer the time propagation of the velocity, the test values are defined as the variables at time \(t\), and the training values as those of time \(t-1\).
The appropriate prior can be re-written as following.

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{u^{t}} \\
        \boldsymbol{p^{t}} \\
        \boldsymbol{u^{t-1}}\\
        \boldsymbol{p^{t-1}} \\
        \boldsymbol{G^{t}} \\
        \boldsymbol{s^{t}}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,
    \begin{bmatrix}
         \boldsymbol{K_{\star \star}} & \boldsymbol{Q}\\
        \boldsymbol{Q^T} & \boldsymbol{K} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right)
    \label{eq:joint-generic}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{K} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t-1}, u^{t-1}}} &\boldsymbol{K_{u^{t-1}, p^{t-1}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t-1}, G^t}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t-1}, s^t}} \\
        \ & \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t-1}, p^{t-1}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t-1}, G^t}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t-1}, s^t}} \\
        \ & & \boldsymbol{K_{G^t, G^t}} & \boldsymbol{K_{G^t, s^t}} \\
        \ & & & \boldsymbol{K_{s^t, s^t}} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:Ktrain-generic}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{Q} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, u^{t-1}}} &\boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, p^{t-1}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, G^{t}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, s^t}} \\
        \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t}, u^{t-1}}} &\boldsymbol{K_{p^{t}, p^{t-1}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t}, G^{t}}}& \boldsymbol{K_{p^{t}, s^t}} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:Q-generic}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{K^{\star \star}} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, u^{t}}} &\boldsymbol{K_{u^{t}, p^{t}}} \\
        &\boldsymbol{K_{p^{t}, p^{t}}} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent where, \(s^t\) is defined as the incompressibility of velocity at time \(t\).

Since all of these variables are linearly related through the unsteady Stokes equation and
continuity equations, every correlation function can instead be expressed in terms of \(d(d+1)/2\) velocity--velocity correlations \(k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}\), \(d\)
velocity--pressure correlations \(k_{u^{t}_\alpha p}\), and a single pressure--pressure
correlation function \(k_{pp}\), where \(d\) is the spatial dimension. For the two-dimensional flows considered here (\(d=2\)), this
amounts to three velocity--velocity kernels (\(k_{u^{t}_x u^{t}_x}, k_{u^{t}_x u^{t}_y}, k_{u^{t}_y u^{t}_y}\)),
two velocity--pressure kernels (\(k_{u^{t}_x p}, k_{u^{t}_y p}\)), and the single pressure--pressure
kernel \(k_{pp}\). Note that \({G^t}\) is as a linear combination of the unknown \(u^t\) and \(p^t\), thus, we can derive the kernel function of \({G^t}\) as
kernel function of \(u^t\) and \(p^t\). All the correlation functions are derived as follows

\begin{align}
k_{u^{t}_\alpha G^{t}_\beta} &= k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} + \Delta t \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha p^{t}} - \Delta t \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} \\
k_{u^{t}_\alpha s^{t}} &= \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} \\
k_{p^{t} G^{t}_\alpha} &= k_{u^{u}_\alpha p^{t}} + \Delta t \mathcal{D}'^\alpha k_{p^{t}p^{t}} - \Delta t \mathcal{L}' k_{p^{t} u^{t}_\alpha} \\
k_{p^{t}s^{t}} &= \mathcal{D}'^\alpha k_{p^{t} u^{t}_\alpha} \\
\begin{split}
k_{G^{t}\alpha G^{t}_\beta} &= k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 + {\Delta t} \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha p^{t}}
 - {\Delta t} \mathcal{L} k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 + {\Delta t} \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha p^{t}}
 + {\Delta t}^2 \mathcal{D}^\alpha \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{p^{t}p^{t}} \\
&\qquad - {\Delta t}^2 \mathcal{D}'^\beta \mathcal{L} k_{u^{t}_\alpha p^{t}}
 - {\Delta t} \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 - {\Delta t}^2 \mathcal{D}'^\alpha \mathcal{L} k_{u^{t}_\beta p^{t}}
 + {\Delta t}^2 \mathcal{L} \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} 
\end{split} \\
k_{G^{t}\alpha s^{t}} &= \mathcal{D}^\alpha \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{p^{t} u^{t}_\beta}
 - \Delta t  \mathcal{L} \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} \\
k_{s^{t}s^{t}} &= \mathcal{D}^\alpha \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} \\
\end{align}

\noindent where the prime symbol (') indicate that the operation should is performed on the second argument; 
eg, \((\mathcal{D}'^{\alpha}k)({\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}}) = \partial_{x_\alpha} k_{\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}}\)
and \((\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}k)({\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}}) = \partial_{y_\alpha} k_{\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{y}}\)

The general posterior is then

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \boldsymbol{u^{t}} \\
    \boldsymbol{p^{t}} \\
\end{bmatrix}
\bigg|
\begin{bmatrix}
    \boldsymbol{u^{t-1}} \\
    \boldsymbol{p^{t-1}} \\
    \boldsymbol{{G}^{t}} \\
    \boldsymbol{s^t} \\
\end{bmatrix}
\sim\mathcal{N}\left(
    \boldsymbol{Q K^{-1}} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{u^{t-1}} \\ \boldsymbol{p^{t-1}} \\ \boldsymbol{{G}^{t}} \\ \boldsymbol{s^t} \\\end{bmatrix}, 
    \boldsymbol{K^{\star \star}} - \boldsymbol{Q K^{-1} Q^T}
    \right)
    \label{eq:posterior-generic}
\end{equation}

\section{Uncorrelated Velocity and Pressure Priors}%
\label{sec:zero-up}
 
The six base kernels above allow the prior to encode an \emph{a priori} correlation between
the velocity and the pressure through \(k_{u^{t}_x p}\) and \(k_{u^{t}_y p}\). In this work these two
cross-kernels are set to zero,
 
\begin{equation}
    k_{u^{t}_\alpha p}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}') = k_{p u^{t}_\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}') = 0,
    \qquad \alpha \in \{x, y\},
    \label{eq:zero-up}
\end{equation}
 
\noindent i.e.\ velocity and pressure are modelled as independent Gaussian processes \emph{before}
any data are observed. This does not mean that velocity and pressure are physically unrelated --
the pressure gradient drives the flow. The coupling between the two is instead supplied by the
physics constraints: the momentum rows \(\mathcal{G}^\alpha\) contain both
\(\mathcal{D}^\alpha p\) and \(\mathcal{L}u^{t}_\alpha\), and the continuity rows \(s\) constrain \(\boldsymbol{u^{t}}\).
Once the GP is conditioned on these rows, the posterior velocity and pressure are correlated even
though the prior treats them as independent. The assumption is therefore that the prior
cross-correlation carries little information that is not already imposed by the governing equations.
Independent priors for coupled fields are also the choice made by \textcite{Raissi2018} for
systems of equations.
 
With\eqref{eq:zero-up}, the derived kernels simplify to
 
\begin{align}
k_{u^{t}_\alpha G^{t}_\beta} &= k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta} - \Delta t \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}, \\
k_{p^{t} G^{t}_\alpha} &= \Delta t \mathcal{D}'^\alpha k_{p^{t}p^{t}}, \\
k_{p^{t}s^{t}} &= 0, \\
k_{G^{t}_\alpha s^{t}} &= \mathcal{L} \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}, \\
\begin{split}
k_{G^{t}_\alpha G^{t}_\beta} &= k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 - \Delta t \mathcal{L} k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 + \Delta t^2 \mathcal{D}^\alpha \mathcal{D}'^\beta k_{p^{t}p^{t}} \\
&\qquad - \Delta t \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
 + \Delta t^2 \mathcal{L} \mathcal{L}' k_{u^{t}_\alpha u^{t}_\beta}
\end{split}
\end{align}
 
\noindent while \(k_{u^t_\alpha s^t}\) and \(k_{s^t s^t}\) are unchanged. Only four base kernels remain,
\(k_{u^t_x u^t_x}\), \(k_{u^t_x u^t_y}\), \(k_{u^t_y u^t_y}\) and \(k_{pp}\), each with three hyperparameters
\((\gamma, l_x, l_y)\), so the number of hyperparameters is reduced from 18 to 12.
This has three practical consequences:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the Nelder--Mead search runs in a 12-dimensional rather than an 18-dimensional space,
          which matters because the number of iterations per step is limited;
    \item roughly half of the derivative kernels that involve \(k_{u^t_\alpha p}\) no longer need to be
          evaluated, which reduces the cost of building \(\boldsymbol{K}\);
    \item with the velocity and pressure blocks independent, the prior over \((\boldsymbol{u^t}, p)\) is a
          valid covariance whenever the velocity block and the pressure block are valid individually,
          which removes one way in which the joint kernel can fail to be positive semi-definite.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Application to Periodic Channel Flow}
\label{sec:periodic-channel}

\textcite{Molina2023} have also shown that with periodic boundary conditions for the velocity u, together with a constant
pressure difference \(\Delta p\) between the inlet \( (x=0) \) and outlet \( (x=L) \)

\begin{align}
    u^t_\alpha(x+L,y) &= u^t_\alpha(x,y) \\
    p(x+L,y) &= p(x,y) + \Delta p.
\end{align}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent These can be expressed as \((S^1_{L} u^{\alpha})(0,y) = 0\) and \((S^1_{L} p)(0,y) = -\Delta p.\)
For the periodic channel-flow geometry considered in this work, the generic training
set of Equations~\eqref{eq:Ktrain-generic}--\eqref{eq:Q-generic} is specialized as
follows: the full-field \(u^{t-1}\) and \(p^{t-1}\) training data are replaced by the
time-invariant no-slip wall velocity \(\boldsymbol{u_b}\) and the periodicity
constraints \(S^1_L\boldsymbol{u}\), \(S^1_L\boldsymbol{p}\), while \(\mathcal{G}^t\) and
\(s\) are retained. Information about \(u^{t-1}\) enters the inference only through
\(\mathcal{G}^t\) (Equation~\eqref{eq:G-rhs}), not as a separate training block.
\(\boldsymbol{Q}\) and \(\boldsymbol{K^{t-1,t-1}}\) are redefined analogously to
Equations~\eqref{eq:Ktrain-generic} and \eqref{eq:Q-generic}, with \(u^{t-1}, p^{t-1}\)
replaced by \(u_b\), \(S^1_L\boldsymbol{u}\), and \(S^1_L\boldsymbol{p}\). The prior
becomes

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        u^{t} \\
        u_b\\
        S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{u} \\
        S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{p} \\
        \mathcal{G}^{t} \\
        s
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{K^{t, t} + \sigma_{t,t}^2 I} & \boldsymbol{Q}\\
        \boldsymbol{Q^T} & \boldsymbol{K^{t-1, t-1}+ \sigma_{t-1,t-1}^2 I} \\
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(\boldsymbol{\sigma_{t,t}^2 I}\) is the uncertainty of the value at time \(t\), and 
\(\boldsymbol{\sigma_{t-1,t-1}^2 I}\) is of time \(t-1\) resepectively. Since, the value at time \(t\)
here are noiseless boundary data, the uncertainty is zero.The posterior changes form to the following form.

\begin{equation}
    \begin{matrix}
      \boldsymbol{u^{t}}\\
      \boldsymbol{p^{t}}\\  
    \end{matrix}
    \bigg|
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \boldsymbol{u_b}\\
        S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{u} \\
        S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{p} \\
        \boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^{t}} \\
        \boldsymbol{s}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}\left(
        \boldsymbol{Q K^{-1}} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{u_b} \\ S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{u} \\  S^1_{L} \boldsymbol{p} \\ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^{t}} \\ \boldsymbol{s} \\\end{bmatrix}, 
        \boldsymbol{K^{t, t}} - \boldsymbol{Q K^{-1} Q^T}
    \right)
\end{equation}

\section{Uncertainty propagation}
\label{sec:uncertainty-propagation}

\noindent Following \textcite{Raissi2018}, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^t}\) is built from the previous
step's posterior mean, \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^t} = \boldsymbol{\mu^{t-1}} +
\frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\boldsymbol{f_{ext}}\), rather than from an exactly known value. Its
uncertainty is accounted for by treating the posterior variance of the previous step as
observation noise on the \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^t}\) rows, while all other training
rows (wall, periodicity and continuity) are noiseless:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\boldsymbol{K}} = \boldsymbol{K}
    + \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{0} & \boldsymbol{0} \\
      \boldsymbol{0} & \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma^{t-1,t-1}}\right)
      \end{bmatrix}
    + \varepsilon^2 \boldsymbol{I},
    \label{eq:K-noise}
\end{equation}
where the lower-right block corresponds to \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^t}\). The posterior
at time \(t\) is then
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\mu^t} = \boldsymbol{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}, \qquad
    \boldsymbol{\Sigma^{t,t}} = \boldsymbol{K^{t,t}} - \boldsymbol{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q^T},
    \label{eq:posterior-propagated}
\end{equation}
with \(\boldsymbol{y} = [\boldsymbol{u_b},\ S^1_L\boldsymbol{u},\ S^1_L\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}},\
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}^t},\ \boldsymbol{s}]^T\). Only the diagonal of
\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma^{t-1,t-1}}\) is used. The additional correction term
\(\boldsymbol{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q^T}\)
of \textcite{Raissi2018} is not applied, since the uncertainty of
\(\boldsymbol{\mu^{t-1}}\) already enters through \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}\).

\section{Hyperparameters Optimization}
\label{sec:optimization}

Following \textcite{Molina2023}, the kernel hyperparameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) are
found via a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate rather than full Bayesian
marginalization,

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\theta}^\star = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \left[\log p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{X}, f(\boldsymbol{X}), I)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{X},f(\boldsymbol{X}),I) \propto
p(f(\boldsymbol{X})|\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{\theta},I)\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|I)\), following
the same construction as \textcite{Molina2023}.

We take \(p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|I)\) to be an
independent Jeffreys prior for each hyperparameter,

\begin{equation}
    -\log p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|I) = \sum_{k} \left( \gamma_k + \log l_{x,k} + \log l_{y,k} \right) + \text{const.},
    \qquad k \in \{u^xu^x,\ u^xu^y,\ u^yu^y,\ pp\}.
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.3cm}

\vspace{0.3cm}
The initial hyperparameter values \((\gamma_1, \gamma_2, \log l) = (1.2, 0, -1.2)\)
are taken from \textcite{Molina2023}, where \(\gamma_1\) is the initial variance scale
for the \(u\)--\(u\) and \(p\)--\(p\) correlations, \(\gamma_2\) the initial variance
scale for \(u\)--\(p\) correlations, and \(l\) the initial length scale, shared across
all correlations at initialization. Unlike \textcite{Molina2023}, where \(\gamma_2 = 0\)
is only the starting point of a full optimization run over all hyperparameters, the
velocity--pressure amplitude of 0 does not apply here, since those kernels are removed
(see §2.3).

The 12 hyperparameters are optimized with Nelder--Mead downhill simplex method, limited to
100 iterations with a tolerance of \(10^{-2}\). Each fit is warm-started from the previous step's optimum,
as recommended by \textcite{Raissi2018}. A jitter term is added to the training covariance diagonal for numerical stability.

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{K} \to \boldsymbol{K} + \epsilon^2\boldsymbol{I}, \qquad \epsilon = 10^{-3}.
\end{equation}

\section{Inference Algorithm}
\label{sec:inference-algorithm}

The full time-marching inference procedure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart}.
The fluid starts at rest, so \(\boldsymbol{u}(t=0) = \boldsymbol{0}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0,0} = \boldsymbol{0}\).
The training set, identical in size at every step, consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 62 no-slip points on each wall (124 in total), used for both \(u_b^x\) and \(u_b^y\) (248 rows);
    \item 15 points on the inlet line \(x = 0\) for each of \(S^1_L u^x\), \(S^1_L u^y\) and \(S^1_L \tilde{p}\) (45 rows);
    \item 340 artificial points, each used for \(\mathcal{G}^x\) and \(\mathcal{G}^y\) (680 rows);
    \item 340 continuity points \(s\) on a fixed \(20 \times 17\) grid mapped onto the channel (340 rows);
\end{itemize}
\noindent giving \(N = 1313\) training rows. At each step \(t = 1, \dots, N_t\):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item build \(\boldsymbol{y}\) with \(\mathcal{G}^t = \boldsymbol{\mu}^{t-1} + \Delta t\,\boldsymbol{f}_{ext}\) at the current
          artificial locations, and the noise block \(\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t-1,t-1})\);
    \item re-fit the hyperparameters (Section~\ref{sec:optimization}) and factorize \(\boldsymbol{K}\);
    \item predict the mean and standard deviation of \(u^x\), \(u^y\) and \(\tilde{p}\) on a
          \(120 \times 41\) test grid, used for plots and error measures;
    \item choose the artificial locations for the next step and evaluate the posterior mean and full
          covariance there; these become \(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{t}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t,t}\).
\end{enumerate}
In step 4 the artificial locations are either resampled at every step, as 340 points
drawn without replacement from a fixed pool of 680 locations sampled uniformly in the
channel, or kept fixed for the whole march. Both variants are compared in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Reference Solution and Evaluation Measures}%
\label{sec:reference}
 
\paragraph{Finite-element reference.}
The PIGP predictions are compared with a finite-element (FEM) solution of the same problem, computed with
scikit-fem using Taylor--Hood (P2/P1) elements on a structured \(200 \times 80\) triangular mesh mapped onto the
channel (16\,281 vertices, 32\,000 triangles). No-slip is imposed strongly on the walls, periodicity in \(x\) is
imposed exactly by identifying the degrees of freedom at \(x=0\) and \(x=L\), and one pressure value is fixed.
The unsteady FEM solution uses the same backward Euler scheme and the same \(\Delta t\) as the PIGP, starting from
rest; the steady FEM solution solves the steady Stokes problem on the same mesh.
 
\paragraph{Accuracy.}
At each step, the PIGP mean on the test grid is compared with the unsteady FEM solution \emph{at the same step},
using the mean absolute error and the relative \(L_2\) error of \(u^x\) and \(u^y\).
 
\paragraph{Calibration of the uncertainty.}
The coverage is the fraction of test points at which the PIGP error lies within the predicted 95\% band,
\(|u_{\mathrm{PIGP}} - u_{\mathrm{FEM}}| \le 2\sigma\); a well-calibrated model gives about 95\%.
 
\paragraph{Steady-state time.}
The steady-state time \(t^\ast\) of each method is measured against \emph{its own} final level, so that it reflects
how fast the solution settles rather than how accurate the final state is. For each method, \(t^\ast\) is the first
time at which the maximum of \(u^x\) stays within 1\% of its final value for at least 0.05 time units:
 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\left|\max u^x(t) - u^x_{\max,\infty}\right|}{u^x_{\max,\infty}} < 0.01 .
    \label{eq:steady-criterion}
\end{equation}
 
\noindent For the FEM, \(u^x_{\max,\infty}\) is the maximum of the steady FEM solution. For the PIGP, it is obtained by
extrapolating the geometric decay of the step-to-step changes,
 
\begin{equation}
    u^x_{\max,\infty} = u^x_{\max,N} + d_N \frac{q}{1-q},
\end{equation}
 
\noindent where \(d_N\) is the last change of \(\max u^x\) and \(q\) the median ratio of the last eight consecutive changes.
The accuracy of the PIGP final state is then reported separately as
\((u^x_{\max,\infty,\mathrm{PIGP}} - u^x_{\max,\infty,\mathrm{FEM}})/u^x_{\max,\infty,\mathrm{FEM}}\).
This extrapolation assumes a smooth decay and is only reliable when the artificial locations are fixed.

\chapter{Results}%
\label{ch:results}

This chapter presents the inferred velocity field for pressure-driven, unsteady Stokes
flow in two two-dimensional geometries, using the time-marching physics-informed
Gaussian process (PIGP) developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.
Section~\ref{sec:results-plates} considers flow between two parallel plates, for which
a closed-form analytical solution is used for validation.
Section~\ref{sec:results-sinusoidal} considers flow through a sinusoidal channel,
following the geometry of \textcite{Molina2023}, for which a finite-element (FEM)
solution is instead used as the reference.


\section{Pressure-driven flow between parallel plates}%
\label{sec:results-plates}

For Poiseuille flow between two stationary plates, initially at rest, with a pressure
gradient \(dp/dx\) suddenly applied at \(t=0\), the unsteady Stokes
equations~\eqref{eq:unsteady} admit the closed-form series solution of
\textcite[Sec.~4.3]{Munoz2012}. Writing the velocity as the sum of the steady plane
Poiseuille profile and a decaying transient part, \(u_x(y,t) = u_0(y) + u_e(y,t)\), and
specializing their result to two stationary plates (\(U=0\)) over the domain
\(y\in[-0.5,0.5]\) used in this work (via the shift \(y'=y+0.5\in[0,1]\)), gives

\begin{equation}
    u_x(y,t) = -\frac{y'(1-y')}{2\eta}\frac{dp}{dx}
    \;+\; \frac{1}{\eta}\frac{dp}{dx}
    \sum_{\substack{n=1\\ n\ \mathrm{odd}}}^{\infty}
    \frac{4}{(n\pi)^3}\, e^{-n^2\pi^2 \nu t}\sin(n\pi y'),
    \qquad y' = y+0.5,\quad \nu = \eta/\rho,
    \label{eq:analytical-plates}
\end{equation}

\noindent which satisfies \(u_x(y,0)=0\) and converges to the steady plane Poiseuille
profile as \(t\to\infty\). In this work, \(dp/dx = -12\) and \(\eta=\rho=1\), so that the
steady centreline velocity is \(u_x(0,t\to\infty)=1.5\). This analytical solution
provides a direct, independent check of the PIGP solution.

The simulation used a time step of \(\Delta t = 0.01\) and \(100\) time-marching steps
from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\), with \(340\) artificial training points per step
(Section~\ref{sec:inference-algorithm}). At each step, the training points were
resampled from the set of generated test points.

The relative error is largest at early times (\(2.89\%\) at \(t=0.1\)), where the
transient is steepest, and decreases monotonically to \(0.04\%\) at \(t=1\) as the flow
approaches steady state. The PIGP slightly underpredicts the analytical solution at
all times. Most of this difference is caused by the backward Euler time
discretization rather than by the GP inference: compared with the FEM, which uses the
same time discretization, the mean absolute error of the PIGP is only
\(1.5\times10^{-4}\) at \(t=0.01\) and remains below \(1.7\times10^{-3}\) throughout
the march.

The same holds for the steady-state time. Using the criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:reference} (Equation~\eqref{eq:steady-criterion}), both the PIGP and
the FEM reach steady state at \(t_\star = 0.50\), so the PIGP recovers the
steady-state time of the reference exactly at the time resolution
\(\pm\Delta t = \pm 0.01\). The analytical solution reaches the same criterion slightly
earlier, at \(t \approx 0.47\). Since the PIGP and the FEM agree, this difference is
again attributable to the time discretization, which slows the approach to steady
state in both methods. The final level of the PIGP is \(0.05\%\) below the steady
value of \(1.5\).

Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_ux_plates} shows the evolution of the maximum and mean
predictive uncertainty \(2\sigma_{u_x}\) over the domain. The uncertainty remains
small throughout, with the maximum \(2\sigma_{u_x}\) mostly between
\(2.3\times10^{-3}\) and \(4\times10^{-3}\), roughly three orders of magnitude below
the steady centreline velocity, and it grows slowly with time as the uncertainty of
each step is propagated into the next (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty-propagation}). The
curves are not smooth: both show step-to-step fluctuations, with isolated dips at
\(t = 0.41\) and \(t = 0.88\). The predictive band is well calibrated at early times,
with a coverage of \(100\%\) over the first six steps, but becomes overconfident as
the error accumulates during the transient, falling to about \(37\%\) for \(u_x\) at
\(t \approx 0.2\)--\(0.4\). Unlike in the sinusoidal channel
(Section~\ref{sec:results-sinusoidal}), the coverage partly recovers once the flow
approaches steady state, reaching \(49\%\) at \(t=1\), since the error stops growing
(the mean absolute error remains between \(1.0\times10^{-3}\) and
\(1.6\times10^{-3}\) after \(t \approx 0.3\)) while the uncertainty continues to
increase slightly. The PIGP also predicts a small spurious cross-stream velocity, with
a mean absolute value of about \(1.2\times10^{-3}\) at \(t=1\), where the exact
\(u_y\) is zero.

These results show that the PIGP accurately reproduces the unsteady pressure-driven
flow between parallel plates, including both the transient and the steady-state time,
with errors dominated by the time discretization rather than by the GP inference.

This earlier steady-state time does not indicate faster dynamics. It is a consequence

each method's \emph{own} final level, the PIGP is compared with its extrapolated final

about \(1.3\%\) below the reference solution. In addition, the extrapolation of the
PIGP final level is itself unreliable here. After \(t \approx 0.32\), the
step-to-step changes in \(\max u^x\) are no longer a smooth decay but fluctuate in sign
(\(1.2883\), \(1.2882\), \(1.2880\), \(1.2896\) at \(t = 0.32\)--\(0.35\)), so the ratio
of successive changes, which the extrapolation assumes to be constant, varies by more
than an order of magnitude.

The relative \(L_2\) error in \(u_x\) is \(0.4\%\), \(0.6\%\) and \(1.0\%\) at
\(t=0.13\), \(0.27\) and \(0.40\), respectively
(Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_ux_sinusoidal}), and the mean absolute error grows almost
monotonically over the whole march, from \(2.7\times10^{-4}\) at \(t=0.01\) to
\(5.9\times10^{-3}\) at \(t=0.40\). This growth reflects the accumulation of error
inherent in the time-marching scheme: at each step, the posterior mean of the previous
step is used as artificial training data (Section~\ref{sec:inference-algorithm}), so
any shortfall in the inferred velocity is carried forward and compounded. The result
is a PIGP solution that increasingly lags behind the FEM. The error grows fastest
after \(t \approx 0.28\): the PIGP velocity has by then nearly levelled off, increasing
by less than \(1\%\) between \(t=0.28\) and \(t=0.40\), while the FEM is still
approaching its higher final level, so the gap between the two continues to widen.
The error at \(t=0.40\) therefore represents a persistent offset between the two
steady states rather than a transient discrepancy, and its magnitude (\(1.0\%\))
agrees with both the \(-1.31\%\) difference in final levels and the underprediction
of the centreline velocity visible in the profiles
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles_sinusoidal}).

The spatial distribution of the absolute error supports this interpretation. At
\(t=0.13\) the error is below about \(10^{-3}\) over most of the channel, whereas by
\(t=0.40\) it reaches about \(10^{-2}\) in the interior, most strongly along the
centreline, where the velocity, and hence any shortfall in its final level, is
largest. The error remains small along the walls at all times, where the no-slip
condition is imposed exactly as training data. The error field is also not symmetric
about the centreline, although the flow itself is; this reflects the random,
non-symmetric placement of the artificial training points.

Errors are larger for \(u_y\) than for \(u_x\), with a relative \(L_2\) error of
\(2.4\%\), \(2.2\%\) and \(4.7\%\) at the same three times
(Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_uy_sinusoidal}), again increasing most after steady state.
This difference is expected on physical grounds rather than being a shortcoming of the
PIGP inference itself: in the sinusoidal channel, \(u_x\) is the dominant velocity
component along the channel axis, while \(u_y\) captures only the smaller cross-stream
motion induced by the curvature of the walls. Since relative error normalizes the
absolute error by the magnitude of the reference field, the same absolute error
corresponds to a substantially larger relative error in \(u_y\), because
\(|u_y| \ll |u_x|\) throughout most of the domain. In absolute terms, the \(u_y\) error
is in fact smaller than the \(u_x\) error (a mean absolute error of
\(2.0\times10^{-3}\) versus \(5.9\times10^{-3}\) at \(t=0.40\)). The same normalization
explains the bright lines in the pointwise relative-error maps of \(u_y\): they
coincide with the centreline and the cross-sections where \(u_y^{\mathrm{FEM}}\)
changes sign, where the denominator vanishes, and not with regions of large absolute
error. For \(u_x\), the relative error is correspondingly largest in a thin layer along
the walls, where \(u_x^{\mathrm{FEM}} \to 0\).

Two features stand out. First, the uncertainty does not track the error. Both the
maximum and the mean \(2\sigma_{u_x}\) grow during the early transient, the mean from
\(1.0\times10^{-3}\) at \(t=0.01\) to about \(2\times10^{-3}\) at \(t \approx 0.1\), but
level off at about \(t \approx 0.2\), with the mean remaining near
\(2.3\times10^{-3}\) thereafter. The mean absolute error, in contrast, continues to
grow over the whole march (Table~\ref{tab:sinusoidal-diagnostics}), reaching
\(5.9\times10^{-3}\) at \(t=0.40\), about \(2.6\) times the mean \(2\sigma_{u_x}\).
Consequently, the coverage falls from about \(93\%\) for both components at the first
step, close to the nominal \(95\%\), to \(22\%\) for \(u_x\) and \(57\%\) for \(u_y\)
at \(t=0.40\): the predictive band is well calibrated at early times but becomes
increasingly overconfident. This is consistent with the nature of the error described
above. The accumulated error is a systematic offset carried forward through the
artificial data, whereas the posterior variance at each step only accounts for the
uncertainty of the current inference given its training data, and so does not
represent the bias inherited from earlier steps.

Second, the uncertainty does not evolve smoothly. Fluctuations are present from
\(t \approx 0.12\) onwards, and after \(t \approx 0.23\) the maximum
\(2\sigma_{u_x}\) alternates between about \(7\times10^{-3}\) and
\(1\times10^{-2}\) from one step to the next. These fluctuations have the same origin
as those in \(\max u^x\) noted above: at every step, a new set of artificial locations
is drawn, so the regions that are well or poorly constrained change from one step to
the next. Their onset coincides with the approach to steady state, when the physical
change per step becomes small and the step-to-step noise from resampling is no
longer masked by it. This noise sets a floor below which changes in the solution
cannot be resolved, and it is this noise floor that makes the extrapolated final
level, and therefore \(t_\star\), unreliable. To remove this source of noise, the next
section repeats the simulation with the artificial points held at fixed locations for
the whole march.

\section{Effect of Fixed Points}%
\label{sec:fixed-points-comparison}

The simulation of Section~\ref{sec:results-sinusoidal} was repeated under identical
conditions (\(\Delta t = 0.01\), 40 steps, 340 artificial points per step), but with
the artificial training points held at fixed locations. In this \emph{fixed-points}
variant, the 340 artificial locations are drawn once at \(t=0\) from the candidate
pool and reused at every step, so that the posterior mean and covariance at step \(t\)
are evaluated at exactly the same locations as at step \(t-1\)
(Section~\ref{sec:inference-algorithm}, step~4). In the \emph{resampled} variant of
Section~\ref{sec:results-sinusoidal}, a new set of locations is drawn at every step.
Figures~\ref{fig:evolution_ux_sinusoidal_fp} and~\ref{fig:evolution_uy_sinusoidal_fp}
show the resulting \(u_x\) and \(u_y\) fields.

with fixed artificial points (\(\Delta t=0.01\), 40 steps, 340 artificial points).
Rows as in Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_ux_sinusoidal}.}%

\end{figure}

The relative \(L_2\) error is \(0.6\%\), \(0.9\%\) and \(1.0\%\) for \(u_x\), and
\(4.4\%\), \(5.3\%\) and \(5.3\%\) for \(u_y\), at \(t=0.13\), \(0.27\) and \(0.40\),
respectively. The most striking feature of the error maps is that the spatial pattern
of the error is essentially frozen: the same structures, such as the closed contour in
the lower-left of the wide section of the \(u_x\) maps, appear at all three times and
only their magnitude changes. In the resampled case
(Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_ux_sinusoidal}), the pattern changes from one snapshot to
the next. This shows directly that, with fixed points, the error is governed by the
placement of the artificial points: regions that are poorly constrained at the first
step remain poorly constrained for the whole march.

The velocity profiles in Figure~\ref{fig:profiles_sinusoidal_fp} lead to the same
conclusion.

The \(u_x\) profiles are almost indentical to earlier case, with the same slight underprediction near the centreline at late times. 
The difference lies in the \(u_y\) profile at \(x=0.9375\). In the resampled variant
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles_sinusoidal}), the PIGP underpredicts the peak magnitude at
\(t=0.16\) and \(t=0.40\) but matches it at \(t=0.32\), so the deviation varies
irregularly from one time to the next. With fixed points, the peak magnitude is
underpredicted at \(t=0.16\), \(0.32\) and \(0.40\) by a similar amount (about
\(-0.076\) versus \(-0.079\) at late times), and the deviation extends over the same
part of the profile (\(0.1 \leq y \leq 0.4\)) at each time. The error at this
location is therefore a persistent, systematic offset rather than a fluctuating one,
consistent with the frozen error pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_uy_sinusoidal_fp}.
It also confirms that the irregular behaviour observed in the resampled case
originates from the resampling of the artificial points. At \(x=1.5625\), the PIGP and
FEM profiles agree closely at all times in both variants.

In contrast to the resampled case (Figure~\ref{fig:uncertainty_sinusoidal}), both the

smoothly to a plateau of about \(25\%\) for \(u_x\) and \(57\%\) for \(u_y\). The same
holds for the solution itself. The step-to-step change \(\max|u^n_x - u^{n-1}_x|\)

exactly the behaviour assumed by the extrapolation of the final level

reaches steady state at \(t_\star = 0.31\), compared with \(t_\star = 0.32\) for the
FEM. This corresponds to a relative difference of \(3.1\%\), equal to a single time
step and therefore within the time resolution of the method, compared with \(12.5\%\)
for the resampled case. The final level itself is also closer to the reference, at
\(-0.93\%\) below the steady FEM compared with \(-1.31\%\).

The comparison reveals two regimes. During the transient, the fixed-points variant is
less accurate: at \(t=0.27\), its mean absolute error is about \(1.6\) times larger for
\(u_x\) and \(2.7\) times larger for \(u_y\). This is consistent with spatial
coverage. When the locations are resampled, every region of the channel is eventually
sampled, so the error introduced by gaps in the training set moves from step to step
and does not accumulate at any one location. With fixed locations, the same regions
remain unsampled throughout the march, and the error there accumulates, as the frozen
error pattern shows.

After steady state, however, the behaviour reverses. The error of the fixed-points
variant saturates together with the flow: its mean absolute error in \(u_x\) changes
by less than \(2\%\) between \(t=0.33\) and \(t=0.40\). The error of the resampled
variant keeps growing, by about \(35\%\) between \(t=0.32\) and \(t=0.40\), because
each new set of locations continues to perturb the solution even once the physical
change per step has become small. By \(t=0.40\), the two variants have comparable
errors, with the fixed-points variant slightly more accurate in \(u_x\) and slightly
less accurate in \(u_y\), and a final level closer to the reference.

The calibration is similar for both variants at \(t=0.40\), with a coverage of about
\(22\)--\(25\%\) for \(u_x\) and \(57\%\) for \(u_y\). The overconfidence discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:results-sinusoidal} is therefore not caused by resampling, but is a
property of the time-marching inference itself, in which the bias inherited from
earlier steps is not represented in the posterior variance.

Since the steady-state time is a central quantity in this work, the fixed-points
variant is used for the remainder of this chapter. Its accuracy is lower only during
the transient, it is comparable at steady state with a more accurate final level, and
its smooth evolution allows \(t_\star\) to be determined reliably.

\subsection{Effect of Artificial Training Data Size}
\label{sec:results-npoints}

To test the effect of the number of artificially generated training points per step,
the sinusoidal-channel case was repeated with \(N_{\mathrm{art}} = 150\), \(220\),
\(270\), and \(340\), holding \(\Delta t = 0.05\) and 10 loops fixed. The relative
\(L_2\) errors are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:npoints}.

The initial expectation was that a larger number of training points would yield
monotonically higher accuracy. The results support this only across the lower part
of the range, and show clear saturation above \(N_{\mathrm{art}} \approx 220\).

At \(N_{\mathrm{art}} = 150\), the method performs poorly: the mean \(u_x\) error of
\(3.94\%\) is roughly an order of magnitude larger than at any higher setting, and
the mean \(u_y\) error of \(17.6\%\) is three times larger. Inspection of the
predicted \(u_y\) fields themselves (Figure~\ref{fig:evolution_uy_150}) shows this is
not merely a loss of numerical precision: at \(t=0.4\) the predicted vortex structure
is visibly distorted relative to the FEM reference, indicating that 150 points is
below the threshold required to constrain the governing equations over this geometry
at all.

Above this threshold the returns diminish sharply. Increasing from 150 to 220 points
reduces the mean \(u_x\) error from \(3.94\%\) to \(0.52\%\), but the further
increases to 270 and 340 points change it only to \(0.40\%\) and \(0.44\%\). The same
holds for \(u_y\), where 220, 270 and 340 points give mean errors of \(5.94\%\),
\(5.28\%\) and \(5.74\%\) respectively. In both components the three larger settings
differ by less than the snapshot-to-snapshot variation within any single run, so they
should be regarded as equivalent in accuracy rather than ranked. Beyond roughly 220
points, the quantity of artificial training data is no longer the accuracy-limiting
factor for this problem.

A separate pattern is visible across the columns rather than the rows. in at least one component of each run: it is the maximum of its row
for both components at 220 points (\(0.9\%\) and \(13.9\%\)) and at 340 points
(\(0.6\%\) and \(9.5\%\)), and the joint maximum in \(u_y\) at 270 points
(\(6.3\%\)). Since this occurs independently of training set size, and in both
velocity components, it is attributable to the state of the flow at that instant
rather than to any one run: \(t=0.3\) falls within the transient during which the
flow accelerates through the narrowest section of the channel, where the velocity
gradients are sharpest and hardest for a finite-length-scale kernel to resolve. The
150-point run does not follow this pattern, but its errors are dominated by its
overall failure to resolve the field and so are not informative here.

On this basis, \(N_{\mathrm{art}} = 270\) is a reasonable working choice for
subsequent simulations. It matches the accuracy of the 340-point run in both
components while using approximately \(80\%\) of the training points, and it is the
setting least affected by the difficult \(t=0.3\) step. The practical benefit is a
smaller covariance matrix to factorize at every time step.

These conclusions rest on a single geometry, a single time step, and a horizon of
\(t \le 0.5\). Whether the saturation point shifts with channel geometry, with
\(\Delta t\), or over longer integration times, where accumulated time-marching
error may place different demands on the training set, remains to be established,
and simulations over a longer time frame are needed.
